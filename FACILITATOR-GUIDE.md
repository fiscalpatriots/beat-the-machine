# Running Second Pass: Beat the Machine in Forty Minutes

A facilitator's guide for a chapter meeting, a classroom, or a firm's staff training.

The game takes ten minutes to play and the debrief is what teaches. A room that plays and leaves
has had a diversion. A room that plays and then answers six questions out loud has learned a
method it can use on the next memo that lands on its desk. Everything below assumes forty minutes
and one facilitator.

## What you need

| Item | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| The link, https://fiscalpatriots.github.io/beat-the-machine/ | The whole game. Nothing to install, no account, and it runs on a phone. |
| A phone or a laptop for each person | Each player runs their own round at their own pace. Two people on one screen produces one set of calls and no useful data. |
| The response sheet, if you want numbers | Every round posts to a Google Form, and the sheet behind that form is the record. You need to own that form to read the sheet. |
| A room where people can talk | The fifteen minute debrief is the reason the session is worth forty minutes instead of ten. |
| Second-Pass-Review-Protocol.pdf, optional | One page to hand across a desk at the close, for anyone who asks what this looks like in a real month end. |

If you do not own the response sheet, run the session off the public link anyway and take the
debrief off the room rather than off data. Ask for a show of hands on each of the six questions
and write the counts on a whiteboard. You lose the by player and by error type breakdown and you
keep everything the session was for. To own the sheet, rebuild the Google Form under your own
account and repoint the form identifiers in the game, which the repository's README explains under
"If the form is ever rebuilt".

## Before the session

**Send the link the day before, with one line asking for a single run.** Doing this puts the
scores in the sheet before you walk into the room, so the debrief can name real numbers instead of
estimates. The cost is that the fourteen lines are no longer new, so the twelve minutes in the
room become a replay of the hardest lines on a shared screen rather than a cold round. Decide
which you want. The minutes below assume the room plays cold and the link goes out afterwards to
anyone who missed it.

**Ask for one run each, not two.** A second run posts a new row under the same codename, and a
player who has already seen the answers scores high for the wrong reason.

**Choose a codename rule and say it in the invitation.** Each player picks two words on the way in
and those two words are the only thing the sheet carries about them. A rule makes the leaderboard
readable and keeps the room anonymous. An adjective and an animal works. So does two words from
the player's own work. The rule that matters is that the same person uses the same codename every
time, because that is how a replay is told from a new player.

## The session plan

| Minutes | Segment | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | Framing | You say what the memo is and what the threshold rule is, and nothing else. |
| 12 | Play | Each person plays the fourteen Halyard lines alone. Par is ten minutes. |
| 3 | Fresh case | Five lines of a company nobody has seen, scored on their own. |
| 15 | Debrief | Six questions in order, two answers from the room on each. |
| 5 | Close | The numbers back to the room, and what this looks like in a real close. |

**Framing, five minutes.** Say three things and stop. A month end close produces a ledger. The
commentary you are about to read is a built case, written the way an AI assistant writes close
commentary and carrying the mistakes they make, and it reads well. You are the second pass on it.
Then read the threshold rule out loud, because it decides which lines owe an
explanation at all: a movement owes commentary only when it clears both more than $25,000 and at
least 10 percent of the prior balance, both legs and not either one. Do not name the six ways a
memo fails before they play. Naming them is handing over the answer.

**Play, twelve minutes.** Phones or laptops out, nothing on the projector, and no questions
answered about a specific line while the round is running. Par is ten minutes and the game shows
each player their own clock. The two extra minutes are for the people who read slowly, and you
will need them.

**Fresh case, three minutes.** The round does not end at line fourteen. Five more lines follow,
from a four office dental group the player has never opened, scored separately. Say only what the
bridge screen says, which is new company, new memo, same job. The whole point of the fresh case is
that nobody coached them through it, so coaching them through it destroys the measurement.

**Debrief, fifteen minutes.** The script is below. Roughly two minutes a question.

**Close, five minutes.** Give the room whatever numbers you have. Then say the part that outlives
the game: four of the six failures are machine checkable and two are not. Arithmetic, direction,
threshold and silence can all be recomputed by a script, and the checker on the same site does
exactly that on any ledger and memo pasted into it. Invented driver and timing need the facts on
file and somebody who knows the business, so those two stay with a person. That is the whole
argument, and five minutes is enough to make it.

## The debrief script

Ask the question. Take two answers from the room before you say anything. Then give the tell,
which is the sentence a player can carry to the next memo they review.

| Order | Ask the room | Error type | Lines | The tell |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Which figure in this memo did not tie to the ledger? | Arithmetic | 1 and 8 | The memo handed you a number, so check it against the ledger before you read the reason attached to it. Freight billed fell $65,000 and the memo said $6,500. Bad debt moved $47,000 and the memo accounted for $42,000. |
| 2 | Which sentence has the movement running the wrong way? | Wrong direction | 3 | The memo used the word declined next to a June balance that is higher than May's. Inbound freight rose $73,800. |
| 3 | Which two sentences cannot both be true? | Timing | 2 | The same roof and dock leveler program is described as finished on June 30 and as still running into August. Which one holds decides whether June owes an accrual. |
| 4 | Which reason sounded specific and rested on nothing? | Invented driver | 7 | Ask what document would prove the sentence. When the answer is that nothing in the file would, it is a guess wearing a fact's clothes. |
| 5 | Where did the memo explain one account by pointing at another? | Wrong account | 12 and 14 | When the memo names another account, go and look at that account. Depreciation did not move at all, and the revolver drawn on June 3 is the change nobody mentioned. |
| 6 | Which account owed an explanation and never got a sentence? | Silence | 11 | Read the ledger for what the memo left out, not only for what it got wrong. |

**Question six is the one the room usually misses.** In the sample findings the silent line was
called correctly by 77.8 percent of players, against 88.9 percent on the lines where a sentence
was visibly wrong. The reason is mechanical rather than a failure of attention. A reviewer reads
the memo and checks the memo, and warehouse wages at $72,500 and 11.5 percent clears both legs of
the threshold and appears nowhere in it. The only way to see a silent line is to work from the
ledger to the memo instead of from the memo to the ledger. Say that out loud, because it is the
single habit most likely to change what somebody does on Monday.

**Close the debrief on the clean lines.** Six of the fourteen are sound and the careful call is to
leave them alone. Ask which line somebody flagged that they should have let stand, and take one
answer. A flag on a clean line is not a harmless excess of caution. It spends a controller's
afternoon producing a document for a movement that owed nothing, and a reviewer who flags all
fourteen lines scores eight out of fourteen and ranks at the bottom, which is what the key is
built to do.

## How to read the response sheet in five minutes

Open the form's Responses tab and choose File, then Download, then comma separated values.

**With the script.** Run `findings.py` against the file you just downloaded. It writes a dated
findings document with the catch rate by error type, by card, by player and by organization, the
false flag rate, the lap times and the fresh case transfer rate, plus two short paragraphs written
from the real numbers that are ready to paste into a write up. It matches columns by the form's
question titles rather than by position, and any title it cannot find is printed under "Columns
not found" and the measure that needed it is skipped rather than guessed.

**By hand.** Three numbers, in this order.

1. **Catch rate on the eight problem lines.** Lines 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 11, 12 and 14 carry a planted
   defect. Count correct calls over calls seen. Card one is inverted on purpose, because its
   question asks whether the player agrees that nothing is owed on account 4200, so flagging that
   line posts Reject while flagging any other line posts Accept.
2. **False flags on the six clean lines.** Lines 4, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 13 are the denominator, and a
   flag on any of them is a false flag. This is the control, and a session that reports a catch
   rate without it is reporting half the result.
3. **The fresh case rate.** The column for question C opens with `Round2: 4/5`, followed by the
   five calls and the five keyed answers as letters and the round two clock in seconds. Average
   the fraction and set it beside the main score written the same way. A player at 12 of 14 and 4
   of 5 read 85.7 percent trained against 80.0 percent fresh, and a gap near zero says the method
   carried over rather than the answers.

Two housekeeping rules before any of those numbers mean anything. Count distinct codenames rather
than rows, because Play again posts a new row under the same codename. And drop your own test rows
first, which is why a test codename should say so in the name.

One more thing to filter. Four questions the form still marks required are no longer asked on
screen and post fixed values on every submission, so they are not player answers and must not be
read as opinion. The cell for question B says so in its own text.

## What to do with the numbers

**Report them back to the room, the same week.** Give the catch rate by error type, the false flag
rate and the median lap time. Name the top three codenames and no others, because the game already
promised that and promised nothing more.

**Keep them for the next cohort.** One dated findings document per session with the downloaded
file beside it. The measurements are worth more as a series than as a single reading.

**Compare cohorts, carefully.** The same fourteen lines and the same key make two sessions
comparable. Rows filed before 12 September 2026 were scored against an earlier key with a
different split of problem lines to clean lines, and they are not comparable with anything filed
since. Note the key date beside every set of numbers you keep.

## Variations

**Run the checker live on a memo the room writes.** Bring a variance schedule of your own, real or
invented, and give an AI assistant the drafting prompt: "Here is a month end variance schedule for
June. Write one sentence of commentary for each account that moved, naming a cause for each one."
That prompt is the one most people actually use, and it produces the fluent, confident, partly
unsupported memo the game is built around. Paste the schedule and the memo the assistant returns
into `checker.html` on the same site and run the four mechanical checks on the projector. Fails
and silent lines appearing on material the room made ten minutes ago lands harder than any case.

**Run it as a timed individual assessment.** Par is ten minutes, no talking, one run each, and the
result is the score out of fourteen with the lap time and the false flag count beside it. The
ranks are Trainee at eight or fewer, Staff at nine or ten, Senior at eleven or twelve, Manager at
thirteen and Partner at fourteen. Tell people in advance that flagging everything is a losing
strategy, because it is, and a player who does it scores eight and lands at Trainee.

**Run it with the protocol as a controls exercise.** Hand out `PROTOCOL.md`, split the room into
preparer, second pass reviewer and controller, and work one memo through the four mechanical
checks and the two judgment questions with `EVIDENCE-LOG-TEMPLATE.csv` open. The exercise is not
the arithmetic. It is who may not hold which role, what an ask to the controller looks like when a
figure cannot be supported, and exactly which words a signature covers.

## For instructors

Four learning objectives, stated the way a student can repeat them.

1. **Recompute before you read.** A figure inside a sentence is a claim about the ledger. Testing
   it takes ten seconds and it settles the sentence before any judgment is required.
2. **A reason is a claim.** A named driver is worth exactly as much as the document behind it. A
   driver with nothing on file has not been supported, however specific it sounds.
3. **Silence is a finding.** An account that owes commentary and carries no sentence is a finding
   in its own right, and it is invisible to anyone who reads the memo instead of the ledger.
4. **A clean line is a decision too.** Leaving a sound line alone is review work, and a flag on a
   clean line spends somebody's time where there is no money.

Assessment comes off the response sheet without any hand coding: the score out of fourteen, the
false flag rate on the six clean lines, and the fresh case rate on five lines of a company the
student never saw. The last of those is the one that distinguishes learning from recall, because
the fresh case carries three of the same error types on a different industry with no coaching in
between.

No prerequisite beyond knowing what a variance schedule is. The threshold rule is stated on the
screen and again in the framing, and nothing else in the case assumes prior close experience.

Halyard Provisioning Group does not exist and no figure in the case came from any real company or
engagement. `PROVENANCE.md` in this repository records how the case and its memo were built. Read
it before you run the session, because the room has to be told that the memo is constructed rather
than captured: it was written the way an AI assistant writes close commentary, with the failures
planted so they can be taught and scored.
